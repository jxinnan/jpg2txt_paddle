godeatgod

A layered and moving exploration of power, sexuality, spirituality and survival in the post traumatic world, this multimedia, multiracial and multinational romp is interwoven with the stories and emotional landscape of its characters.

godeatgod is an intimate, personal and at times funny exposition about trying to live a better life; a take on censorship in the context of art; and an examination of love and grief.

 POLITICAL THEATRE STRAND I

I’m going to tell you the story of a hero that I have great admiration for. This man was imprisoned for about 10 years before going on exile because he was considered the foremost political opponent of the Marcoses. You all know who they are.

Well, one day in August 1983 he said, ‘Enough is enough. I’m going home. I’m going to face the enemy.’ So he boarded a plane and sat in economy class under a disguised name. But everyone in the plane knew him. Passengers wanted their pictures taken with him. Some even got his autograph. It turned into a small party in the sky.

Meanwhile, back home, people wearing yellow ribbons were waiting to give him a warm welcome.

Page 191

Just before the plane landed, the man secretly took out his rosary and started saying a prayer. He knew that his life would be in danger once he arrives. The plane landed in Manila and was halted. Three uniformed men entered the plane and singled him out. There was sudden confusion. Those who knew him offered to accompany him along just to make sure he’d be safe. But the three men insisted that he was to be led off alone. So they brought him out and shut the plane door. And immediately after, shots were heard.

Everyone knew he was dead. His body, drenched in blood, lay on the ground. He was wearing white. He died finally kissing the soil of his native land. The man had prepared a speech hoping to read it upon his arrival. But he never did. Part of it goes like this:

‘I return from exile to offer faith in our people and faith in God. I am prepared to die because the Filipino is worth dying for.’

Three years after, the Marcoses were overthrown and the man’s wife eventually became President. But until today, the mastermind behind the assassination remains a secret. All that we ever got to do to remember him was put his face in our money bills and name the airport where he died after him. I will always remember him, his unrelenting faith in the Filipino. His name is Ninoy Aquino. Thank you.

 POLITICAL THEATRE STRAND II

I want to share with you a story. On one hand, it’s a story of courage and determination. And on the other, it represents the worst kind of tyranny and injustice.

One day in June 1989, a young man stood in front of 17 moving tanks. When the tank moved left, he too moved left. When the tank moved right, he too moved right. It was a magnificent sight.

The tank drivers shouted, ‘Hey, are you crazy!’ The young man replied, ‘Why are you here? My city is in chaos because of you! Go back! Go back!’ The tank drivers grew impatient and shouted, ‘Hey! You crazy! We would crush you!’ The young man replied, ‘Don’t stop me! They destroyed my country! My country, my home!’

Meanwhile, around the gigantic parade square, the masses — students, workers, children, families — camped around for days and nights. They cooked their meals there, they sang songs, they made friends. They were fighting for democracy in China.

Page 195

However, after many weeks of demonstration, the Chinese government lost their patience. They started shooting at the students, the workers, the families. They crushed human bones with their tanks. The army shot them down from behind. Many people fell. Like rabbits shot down by hunters, they dropped to the floor. The following was said by a mother of one of the young men killed. I don’t know if there is a word in English to convey the meaning of the Chinese word “nao” [To constantly suffer]. But I think for myself, and for the other mothers, this word is the most accurate. The tears I shed for my dead son — there is a phrase in Chinese, “using one’s tears to wash one’s face”. In those two years, that was how I lived my life. Those who were killed were all young citizens of Beijing. They were up against an armed military enforcing martial law. They simply wanted to persuade the military not to enter the city, and not to use force against the people. When he was shot he thought that he had been hit by a rubber bullet. Right up to his death, he thought that it was a rubber bullet that pierced his heart, and ended his 17-year-old life. He didn’t think it could have been a real bullet.’ 1989. Whenever I think of the Tiananmen Square massacre, I get angry. I get very, very angry. Thank you.

 EPILOGUE

We have to live our life with compassion. We have to see things in the bigger perspective. Art is important. But sometimes we just have to say what we have to say. No subtext. No metaphors, no symbols… We should say and discuss what we feel, especially about matters close to our hearts… matters that are important to all of us. Even if you leave with no answers, no messages, maybe even a little confused… I hope you will leave with some personal reflection. Thank you.

POSTSCRIPT

Excerpts from

Interlogue—Studies in Singapore Literature Volume 6: Haresh Sharma

godeatgod is a reflection by Sharma, in many ways, on the politics and personal tragedies of a post-9/11 world — a world which, for him, is not a dog-eat-dog world but a godeatgod world. Powerful people, the secular gods, if you like, of the 21st century, are at war over ideologies...

Clarissa Oon recognised in a review, when it was restaged in November 2004, that ‘one of the most important things art can do in the embattled post-9/11 world is not to give the right answers, but to ask the right questions — and to do so with heart’. She went on to say that, ‘Showing how social theatre can have a distinctly Asian voice, godeatgod is also the perfect antidote to rambling or too-glib experimental theatre pieces disconnected from the flesh and blood of human suffering’. (The Straits Times, November 2, 2004)

While the 2004 production moved far away from the localised Singapore references of the 2002 play, Sharma retained the central questions he was posing in the play in 2002: ‘What good is there in the world? What can people do? What is the role of art? Why and how do we grieve over the death of loved ones?’ and so on. Alfian Sa’at described it online by saying that it was ‘no exaggeration to say that recent Singapore theatre has not witnessed a performance that is so aesthetically dense, semiotically luxuriant, and intellectually iridescent’. With God on trial for the murder of millions, a mourning wife dealing with her husband’s terminal illness and death, artists questioning the Tiananmen Square incident, the Bali bombing and others, Sharma seeks to engage with these big universal questions.

 David Birch
