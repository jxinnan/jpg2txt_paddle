UNDERCOVER

PRODUCTION NOTES

Undercover was first produced in Singapore by TheatreWorks in November 1994. The production was directed by Lok Meng Chue. The cast was as follows:

HEAD Lim Kay Tong
DEPUTY Benjamin Ng
QIANG Casey Lim
DOLLY PARTON ONG/JANE Tan Kheng Hua

CHARACTERS

HEAD Head of the Department
DEPUTY Deputy Head of the Department
QIANG Person in charge of the Centre
DOLLY PARTON ONG/JANE Intelligence Officer
FLO A member of the Centre
BAGS A member of the Centre
WILLY A member of the Centre
LINDA A member of the Centre

THE PLAY

SCENE 1 An imaginary country. The present. A government office. Head is at his desk. Deputy enters carrying a file.
HEAD Ah, Deputy. Is this the day when our new man comes?
DEPUTY Yes, sir. And no.
HEAD Yes and no?
DEPUTY That’s right, sir. It’s the day, but it isn’t a man. They hav[e] sent us a woman officer.
HEAD (shaking his head) A woman?
DEPUTY (sadly) Unfortunately, sir.
HEAD A woman intelligence officer. That, Deputy, is what I w[ould] call a contradiction in terms.
DEPUTY Exactly what I told myself, sir.
HEAD Can’t we swop her with a man from a less critical department, like the army, perhaps?
DEPUTY We already tried that, sir. They wrote back to say thanks[, but] no thanks. Not in so many words, of course.
HEAD What were their exact words?
DEPUTY “We have the technology, so we don’t need any women[ here,”] quote, unquote. It looks like we are stuck with her, sir.”
HEAD Anyway, we do have orders to try her out.
DEPUTY I wasn’t aware of that, sir.
HEAD That’s because the only person who’s supposed to kn[ow] this in the department is me. You are not senior enoug[h to] know that.
DEPUTY But now I know, sir.
HEAD Yes, that is because I’ve just told you.
DEPUTY But you just said I am not supposed to be told.
HEAD Yes. But I like you.
DEPUTY You do, sir?
HEAD Yes, quite a lot in fact.
DEPUTY I am worried, sir.
HEAD Have no fear, Deputy, I won’t let my personal feeling[s] get in the way of professional duties. At least not du[ring] office hours.
DEPUTY I didn’t mean that, sir. I meant I hope you have done nothing wrong in revealing to me information which I am not supposed to know.
HEAD Are you questioning my judgement?
DEPUTY Not at all, sir.
HEAD Good. I hope this experiment with women fails.
DEPUTY Why, sir?
HEAD If it succeeds, we might be swamped with women. I have always believed that the real reason we men go to work is to escape from women during daylight hours.
DEPUTY Indeed, sir.
HEAD Besides if we have too many women here, there would be grave administrative problems.
DEPUTY Such as, sir?
HEAD We’ll have to convert half of our gents to ladies. I don’t like having the gents only on alternate floors, Deputy.
DEPUTY It would be a great inconvenience indeed, sir.
HEAD Then there are the management problems.
DEPUTY Management problems?
HEAD Yes! They will be hiding in their toilets half the time. There will be no way of knowing what transpires behind the Ladies sign. And if the management does not know what is going on, how is the management going to manage? Particularly since our department motto is: we make everybody’s business our business.
DEPUTY I see what you mean, sir.
HEAD You know, Deputy, I am quite out of my depth when it comes to women. Tell me, do you have experience “handling” women?
DEPUTY Yes, sir. I handle my wife and mother-in-law daily.
HEAD You must have an interesting home life!
DEPUTY Erm, I—
HEAD That’s a just joke, Deputy, a little joke.
DEPUTY (uncertainly) Ah, yes. Ha, ha.
HEAD So, in the light of your extensive experience, Deputy, how should one operate with respect to these women?
DEPUTY With respect, sir. But not too much. The quality we need above all is patience, sir. They tend to get emotional.
HEAD Temperamental?
DEPUTY Yes, sir.
HEAD Unreliable?
DEPUTY They are a little bit on the shaky side, sir.
HEAD This shakiness, as you put it, has been ascribed to the workings of the hormones, I believe?
DEPUTY That’s right, sir. It can be reduced to a simple question of chemistry, sir.
HEAD Talking of which, have we screened this new officer?
DEPUTY Yes, sir. We have had her estrogens and progesterones checked out.
HEAD Her profile?
DEPUTY Seems promising. (reading from file) Stress resistance factor: higher than average. Commitment to goals: good. Acting ability: acceptable. In a nutshell, sir, her hormones don’t appear to be a handicap. At least under the conditions in which they were tested.
HEAD We can only go so far, Deputy. It’s the performance on the job itself that will sort the sheep from the goats. Does she have any experience?
DEPUTY No, sir.
HEAD Excellent! I don’t like those ex-student informants. Amateurs all. They come into the service with all manner of preconceived ideas, and we have to spend years trying to eradicate them. I like them fresh, Deputy.
DEPUTY This one is, sir. Fresh from the university, that is.
HEAD On the subject of which, I hope she’s not from some second-rate institution?
DEPUTY (checking) I think she’s from the national university, sir.
HEAD That’s what I mean.
DEPUTY (finds entry) No, sir. It says here that she’s from the technological university.
HEAD (rhetorically) And that’s supposed to be better?
DEPUTY I suppose not, sir.
HEAD But honours, at least?
DEPUTY Honours, first class, sir.
HEAD Well, that does compensate somewhat. You know, Deputy, just between you and me—
DEPUTY Yes, sir?—
HEAD Between you and me, sometimes I think that our real job in all this really is that of trying to maximise our manpower allocation notwithstanding its non-optimality.
DEPUTY Maximising our manpower allocation notwithstanding its non-optimality—
HEAD Exactly! It may be a regrettable truth that nature’s laws decree that it’s an impossibility for one to transform a mule into a racehorse. But there is always hope of making it into a good pack animal. After all, without meaning it as an insult to you, Deputy, look what we have done with you: local U¹, no honours, arts degree, and now a Deputy Head of the department. Why, one day you might even replace me!
DEPUTY Yes, sir. I have always appreciated how far I have come in spite of my poor credentials.
HEAD Well?
DEPUTY Yes, sir?
HEAD Show the Glaxo plant in.
DEPUTY (pause, then slowly understanding) Glaxo... Ah, another one of your little jokes again. Ha, ha.
Deputy goes to the door.
DEPUTY Ong!
Jane, a young attractive woman enters.
HEAD (to Deputy, in disappointment) What’s this, Deputy. You didn’t warn me that she’s good looking!
JANE Thank you, sir.
DEPUTY (as he holds up a hand to silence Jane) Regrettably she is, sir.
HEAD Where on earth are all the plain women?
DEPUTY Actually, they are all in the sky, sir. I hear most of them are stewardesses on our national airline.
JANE Is there something wrong with me, sir?
DEPUTY Terribly. You look too good. In our line of work, we must not be too attractive, or for that matter, too ugly. That’s one of the reasons why the Head and I have risen so far.
HEAD Yes, nothing about you must draw notice to yourself. What’s her name?
DEPUTY Dolly Parton Ong, sir.
HEAD What kind of a name is that?
DEPUTY By the way, sir, Dolly Parton is the singer with (he mimes “big buxom”)—
HEAD I know that! What kind of a name is that for an intelligence officer?
JANE I don’t know, sir. I hadn’t realised that it is objectionable.
DEPUTY Highly objectionable, Dolly. Or is it Parton? Your name must be as ordinary as your looks. If it had been something more obscure like Marie Osmond Ong, then it might just have been acceptable.
HEAD She definitely has to change.
DEPUTY Yes, sir, change.
JANE Change, sir?
HEAD Yes, your name, your looks, everything. In fact, if we could change your sex, I would have insisted on that too. Unfortunately, so far in this country, only the army has the technology.
DEPUTY May I suggest, sir. (rearranges Jane’s hair) straight hair, ponytail—single ponytail tied at the back.
HEAD You seem to know a lot about hair.
DEPUTY I wanted to become a hairdresser once, sir. Between the age of 18 and 19. Even went to London to take up a diploma.
HEAD What happened?
DEPUTY Oh, I had to give it up, sir. I was allergic to shampoo. Later I wanted to be a deep-sea diver. But I had to give that up too because the wet suits gave me a rash: I was allergic to rubber.
HEAD That’s enough about you, we can leave these details for your obituary. Now, how about the rest of her?
DEPUTY No makeup, and no more facials. Let’s see... Yes, clothes: jeans and tee shirt and moccasins, with the occasional dress as an extravagance.
JANE But, that’s so boring!—
DEPUTY That’s what my wife looks like, sir.
HEAD Yes, I remember her as a rather forgettable woman. I hope you have taken note, Officer Ong?
JANE But I—
HEAD And the new name?
DEPUTY Jane, sir.
JANE But, sir—
HEAD I knew a Jane once, Jane. Not in the biblical sense, of course. (very pleased at his “joke”) I think it’s suitably plain.
Head looks at Deputy, who forces a laugh.
JANE Yes, sir. But I—
HEAD Just charge the expenses for your transmutation to the department.
JANE I—
DEPUTY (hinting to Jane to stop resisting) I am glad that you are taking this rather well... Jane...
JANE (who is not taking it very well) We were taught in the intelligence school, sir, that we might be required to undergo a restyling upon posting. But—
HEAD That’s the attitude, young girl. It’s settled then?
DEPUTY It’s settled then, sir.
Both of them look intently at Jane, who wavers.
JANE (finally) Yes, sir. I suppose, sir.
DEPUTY Good! Anything else, sir?
HEAD You forgot bridge.
DEPUTY Oh yes, sir. Jane, do you know contract bridge?
JANE The card game? No, sir. I didn’t know that there are subversives in the National Bridge Association.
DEPUTY There may be, but this is not an assignment.
JANE Why bridge then?
DEPUTY Because we play it during lunch here every day.
HEAD And attendance is compulsory.
DEPUTY Last year, we won the inter-department bridge competition—and all of the five years before that. We don’t want to lose the trophy.
HEAD Yes, we take play seriously in our work here, Jane.
JANE I don’t know if I can pick it up, sir.
HEAD (to Deputy) It is not the same chemical problem surfacing again, is it?
DEPUTY I doubt that, sir.
HEAD Um... you are a woman, Jane, but we have faith in you. And you must return our faith by having faith in yourself.
DEPUTY Yes, Jane, women can play bridge.
HEAD It’s just that they cannot play it very well—but we’ll cross that bridge when we come to it.
Head looks at Deputy, who forces a laugh. Deputy looks at Jane, who forces a laugh.
JANE Yes, sir.
HEAD But you will try to learn the game at least, won’t you, Jane? For the department?
JANE Yes, sir, I will. But I disagree that women—
HEAD Good! That’s the attitude.
JANE Thank you, sir. But I don’t think that it’s true that women—
HEAD Then it’s all settled, isn’t it?
DEPUTY It’s all settled, sir.
Both of them look intently at Jane, who wavers.
JANE (finally) Yes, sir. I suppose, sir.
DEPUTY Good! Jane, here are two manuals you will need for your work here.
JANE Thank you, sir. (reads titles) “Procedures for Intelligence Surveillance” and “The ABCs of Contract Bridge”.
DEPUTY Treat them as your Old and New Testaments, Jane.
HEAD Ah, how time flies, 11.45a.m. already. It’s almost lunchtime. Let’s go to the bridge room.
DEPUTY I will join you in a minute, sir.
HEAD Jane?
JANE Yes, sir.
HEAD (as Head and Jane head out) All right, let’s start from the basics. How many cards are there in a pack?
JANE Including the jokers?
HEAD There are no jokers in bridge, Jane. Definitely not in bridge...
Head and Jane exit. Deputy takes out a notebook from his pocket.
DEPUTY (writes) “25 July: He made some sexist remarks, both in absence and presence of new officer Jane. Also continues to exert too much of his energy on bridge. Went to lunch at 11.45 a.m. for the fourth day running. Most importantly, divulged to me official secrets which I am not entitled to be privy to.”
Blackout.
SCENE 2 The Centre. The place has a leftist, shoestring feel. Opens to darkness. Qiang is on the sofa, snoring. A lock clicks. Qiang turns; all is quiet; snores resume. Willy, in all black, switches on a torchlight and shines it squarely on Qiang’s face. Qiang snorts, turns the other way, turns back, and slowly opens his eyes. A moment in which he is unconscious of where he is. Then Qiang jumps up. He is wearing only shorts. Pulls blanket round him. Willy keeps the light trained on Qiang’s face.
QIANG Heh! What’s happening? Get the light out of my eyes! Is it one of your stupid jokes again, Sandy? (searches around for his shirt) Quit fooling around will you, and put the light on! By the way, what time is it?
Silence. Qiang stares into the light.
QIANG Sandy?
Qiang moves towards Willy. Willy switches off the light. Qiang crashes into something.
QIANG Shit! What the—Sandy, for the last time would you put on the bloody!—
From the other side of the room, Flo, also in all black, switches on a torchlight and trains it on Qiang’s face.
... light. Okay, the joke’s over. I just want my shirt... (pause) Who the hell are you guys?
Linda lights a match from point in the room different from Willy, Flo and Qiang. The light flickers for a moment, then Linda lights a cigarette with it. She draws on it and blows the smoke out slowly.
QIANG What do you guys—What the fuck are you doing here?
LINDA (cool but forceful throughout following) Give him his shirt.
Flo throws a shirt to Qiang. Silence.
QIANG I don’t feel like wearing it now. This is my—
LINDA Put the shirt on.
QIANG I won’t bloody do what you tell me. This is my room, godammit.
WILLY Put the shirt on!
LINDA (to Willy) Cool it. Nobody will hurt you. We are from the government.
QIANG I want to see identifications—
LINDA (throws him identification) Naturally—
QIANG (without looking at identification) Do you have a search warrant—
LINDA (throws him search warrant) Fresh and duly signed—
QIANG (without looking at warrant) Look, you have got the wrong man. I have done nothing wrong.
LINDA That is for us to find out. Don’t you even want to check our papers?
QIANG No, no!
LINDA We can understand how you feel, but as I was saying, the more you cooperate, the faster we will be through with this. Now, please put on that shirt.
Qiang is about to protest, but eventually he puts down the broom and wears his shirt.
QIANG All right, what do you want? You better have a good explanation for this.
LINDA Thank you. I am glad you see the logic of my argument. Please sit down.
Qiang slowly does as he is told.
LINDA (to Flo) Let’s get started.
FLO Are you Pang Eng Lau? P-A-N-G E-N-G L-A-U?
QIANG Yes...
LINDA Take your time, Pang. We have lots of it.
FLO Are you a member of the organisation called the Bread Line?
QIANG I am.
FLO Are you the president of this organisation?
QIANG Yes, I am.
FLO Have you been trying to get the members of the Bread Line to... to... Have you been trying to get the members of the Bread Line to start... to start... Have you been trying to get the members of the Bread Line to start... something or other... (sudden shift in voice) Why is it so dark in here? (pause) Shit. (pause) I forgot my lines.
QIANG All right, stop! Lights!
Lights up, to reveal that this is a play in rehearsal.
QIANG Oh hell, must this happen every time, Flo?
FLO Sorry.
LINDA Can’t you ever get it right, Flo? This is the fifth time!
FLO Look, I said sorry already.
QIANG That’s enough for the day everybody. (goes to Flo, hugs her) You have a memory like a sieve, you know that, Flo?
FLO That’s because my mind’s on other things.
Flo tries to hang on to him, but without being rough or hurting, he pulls away.
QIANG (to Willy, who seems to be in deep thought) What’s the matter, Willy?
WILLY I am wondering if this is what really happens when they come to get you.
QIANG Who the hell knows, Willy?
WILLY If we don’t know, then aren’t we misrepresenting things?
QIANG Poetic licence, Willy. Besides, I have set it all in an imaginary country, remember? So no one can accuse us of making things up.
LINDA Well, factual or not, the play works—and that’s what counts, eh?
Linda hugs Qiang—partly for Flo’s benefit as her own, but he pulls away, also not roughly.
QIANG I don’t know. I’m still not happy with it.
Flo hugs Qiang—partly for Linda’s benefit as her own, but he pulls away.
FLO But why, darling?
QIANG I think it’s not funny enough.
BAGS You didn’t write a comedy, that’s why.
QIANG What I am saying is maybe it should have.
WILLY This sounds like it’s going to be even more unreal.
QIANG You see, maybe it’s better to make a joke out of it all. Levity for gravity. I want to make them laugh, Bags.
BAGS Get them entertained!
QIANG Yes. I want to get their guard down. Then when they are really enjoying themselves, they may suddenly realise that this is really not funny at all.
WILLY I don’t know if it is right to make fun of it.
LINDA It won’t get us into trouble, I hope.
QIANG (pause, then a bit too cheerfully) C’mon guys, don’t be so paranoid. Nobody’s been jailed for writing a play before.
BAGS But I don’t wish to be the one setting the precedent.
QIANG But there is another problem with the play. More fundamental. An inherent paradox in the premise of the play.
BAGS A paradox?
QIANG Yes. You see, the play is saying that “they” don’t tolerate dissent, right?
WILLY Well, yes.
QIANG Is this play an example of dissent?
WILLY It is critical of them, no doubt about it. Yes, I would say so.
QIANG So—let’s, for argument’s sake, consider one possible scenario—let’s say they allow our play to be performed.
WILLY You mean they don’t censor it.
QIANG Yes. That would demonstrate that they are not intolerant of the dissent. And since the play’s premise is that they are intolerant, the premise would fail if they let it through.
WILLY Yes. But what if they don’t allow it to be staged?
QIANG By censoring the play, which is an example of dissent, they would be shown to be intolerant of dissent. Then the play’s premise would be borne out.
WILLY Exactly! So the play’s premise works!
QIANG Not quite.
BAGS I’m hungry. Can we go for lunch now?
QIANG Wait! You see, Willy, if the play is banned, there will no play to speak of. If there is no play, then there is no premise. So—
WILLY —if the play’s premise is right, then the play will not exist and there will be no such thing as its premise.
QIANG Right! In a nutshell we have this: if the play is staged, it is meaningless because it does not reflect the truth, and if the play is not staged, it becomes useless as a play because a play must—by definition—be staged before it becomes a play.
WILLY We are stuck with a play which makes no sense either way. A paradox!
QIANG Yes.
BAGS Since everyone is in agreement now, does that mean we can go for lunch?
Jane enters, now looking plain according to order. Her hairstyle, etc. is new. The transformation must be dramatic but instantly recognisable.
JANE Hello, oh hi, I see you are in the middle of something. Maybe I better come back later...
QIANG No, no. Come in. You must be Jane.
JANE Well, I certainly am not Dolly Parton. (laughs)
QIANG (shows “breasts”) Not that you lack the equipment.
JANE Yes, oh, yes, thank you.
QIANG (extending his hand) Qiang.
JANE You look different from what I had expected.
QIANG (teasingly) I know, most people think I am much younger.
JANE (misreading him) No, I don’t mean that. I mean, you look much better than—oh, never mind.
BAGS Watch out for him, Jane. He’s an expert in trapping people into saying things like that.
QIANG Yes, I am a smooth operator.
Jane, who has been holding on to Qiang’s hand, releases it with too much self-consciousness.
QIANG Everybody, meet Jane. And Jane, the members of the Centre. (as they exchange hellos) The big mouth here is none other than Bags. That’s Flo, Linda and Willy. Jane says she wants to help out in the Centre. She’s a civil servant in the Ministry of...?
JANE Internal Affairs. I don’t know if I will be of any help.
FLO Well, we’re putting up a play. I am playing the lead opposite Qiang. (cuddles Qiang) And we need actresses.
LINDA Those who can remember their lines, that is.
FLO Shuddup.
QIANG Have you acted before?
JANE Bit parts, in school. A long time ago.
QIANG Looking at you, that can’t be that long ago.
JANE (flustered) Yes, no, I mean—
BAGS There he goes again. If you really want to know, acting’s very easy.
WILLY That’s Marlon Brando of the East talking.
BAGS Seriously! All you have to do is pretend to be who you are not.
JANE Oh, I’m not really used to that—
WILLY Don’t just stand there taking that in like he’s thrown you some pearl of wisdom!
QIANG Ignore them while you have that luxury. You won’t have the chance once you get to know them. Why don’t you guys carry on first. I’ll show Jane around the place.
BAGS Lunch finally.
All exit except Qiang and Jane. Qiang stares at Jane, assessing her. She stands there rather uncomfortably.
QIANG I had not expected you to be so good looking.
JANE Oh, I’m sorry.
QIANG You sound like somebody has been telling you that being attractive is a fault.
JANE I’m not sure anymore. Isn’t it?
QIANG No, on the contrary. Show me your legs.
JANE Why?
QIANG To see your credentials.
JANE I didn’t know that was a prerequisite to join the Centre.
Qiang indicates with his finger for Jane to lift her hem. Jane does so, but tentatively. Qiang indicates that he wants her to lift it higher.
QIANG Hmm... Very good. I must get better acquainted with them some time.
Jane blushes.
QIANG You know what the problem is, Jane? Most social workers look so plain. Like ugliness is a prerequisite.
JANE You prefer glamour in your helpers.
QIANG Precisely. That is why I don’t think you are using what you have been endowed with, Jane?
JANE I am not used to doing that.
QIANG Ah, it’s a matter of orientation. You need to flaunt it a little, a pretty girl like you. You know, you could look very nice if you dress up a little. (holds up her chin) Put on some makeup, a little rouge to bring out those cheeks, perhaps, and a red—no pink, no not just pink, but rose—yes, definitely a rose lipstick. Show some leg, why not, show plenty of leg. That ought to turn some heads, eh? Never waste your natural assets, Jane.
JANE But I—
QIANG No buts. If you want to help me, you got to listen to me.
JANE But—
QIANG Uh uh, no buts.
JANE (finally) All right...
QIANG It’s settled then?
JANE Yes, it’s settled.
QIANG Good! Let me show you around the place.
Qiang holds out his hand, Jane takes it.
Blackout.

JANE I see, sir.

DEPUTY Sir—
HEAD You don’t have to say a word. I know everything. I have been watching you, Deputy. You and your pitiable notebook, scribbling little notes about me.
DEPUTY Well, in that case, your game is up too. I’ve finessed you. I am under instructions from the Minister to disobey you. Read this.
HEAD I don’t have to. I know what’s inside.

DEPUTY Impossible. The Minister signed it.
HEAD Indeed. But it was I who asked him to do it. When I told him it was for the purpose of ferreting out the betrayers in the department, he was only too glad to cooperate. And so was Jane.
DEPUTY Jane!
HEAD Yes, she gives me a daily update on your shenanigans.
DEPUTY (to Jane) You—
HEAD Double crossover? She has been a singularly good one, Deputy.
JANE Thank you, sir.
HEAD Never underestimate a woman. That’s why we always work so hard at keeping them down.
DEPUTY But all the time you wasted on bridge, is that not an indictment of the way you run the department?
HEAD Wasted? That’s why you will never go far. Don’t you understand that bridge is not a distraction? Don’t you realise that it is the game most amenable to developing the skills of an intelligence officer because it requires you to pass information using code, it requires you to have a straight face, and it requires you to read the mind of your opponent. Now, what better training for our chaps than this. Indeed bridge is as essential to us as golf is to politicians. You probably think that golf is just a game in which grown-up men carrying long sticks chase after small balls on a large field with 18 holes. No, it is where the major decisions of our country are made. People don’t realise that the fate of many depend on the swing of a selected few. Or that policies stand or fall on the achievement of a birdie or bogey. We give our leaders no respect at all. We even go to the extent of making musical jokes about our leaders: the one who can’t sing, the Bee Gees, the Commodores, and The Who. We may laugh at them, but I assure you they are no clowns. People think politicians are ignorant, stupid and ruthless. That is a gross mistake—because only the last is true.
DEPUTY Sir! I hope you allow me to stay on. All I wanted to be was a good mindless bureaucrat. Is that too much to ask? I did my job. I would do anything the department wants me to do. If it’d wanted me to beat up people, I would have gladly done that. If it had wanted me to—
HEAD Please don’t grovel. I hate colleagues who grovel—I have enough detainees doing that already. Anyway, you have the completely wrong conception of what is required of interrogation. You fail to see that torture is a much over-rated instrument. Or let us say torture as normally conceived, you know, a couple of strokes of the rubber hose on the sole, toothpick shoved under the fingernail, electric shock to the nipples, tying up the penis to prevent urination. Now, we don’t go for such crude methods. The most we go for is a very cold room: I like that because once in a while it allows me to dust out the winter clothing I have saved from my Harvard days. Now, torture by my definition can be as subtle as a simple question, to wit, so you love your children very much? Or torture could be as elegant as a pregnant pause, to wit, if the subject asks you the question: is my family all right? A response in the form of a pregnant pause, with or without a smile, is as effective as a sword through the heart as physical pain. Yes, when it comes to torture, my philosophy tends towards that of minimalism.
DEPUTY Sir, just give me one more chance.
HEAD I am afraid it will have to be a no, Deputy. Or shall we say ex-Deputy. You have to leave this office, this department, this building, this minute.
Deputy exits.

HEAD Actually, he doesn’t know yet. He is not senior enough to know yet. Neither are you, actually.
JANE Then why are you telling me?
